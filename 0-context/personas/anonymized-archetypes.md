# Anonymized stakeholder archetypes

Two decision-making archetypes, generalized from real engagement patterns. **Zero
names, companies, locations, or case-study figures appear below or should ever be
added** — these are archetypes for calibrating tone and objection-handling, not case
studies. If a future edit is tempted to add a specific detail "just for color," don't —
generalize further instead.

## Archetype 1 — the founder-visionary

**Profile:** Founder or CEO of a growing company (roughly 100-150 people), personally
hands-on with AI — someone who builds with it themselves, not someone who read about it
in a board deck. Has developed real "AI taste" through personal use, ahead of most of
their own organization.

**What's actually bothering them:** Not competitor fear. It's internal divergence —
leadership is building agentically while most of the org still uses AI at
consumer/copy-paste depth. The gap between "how I work now" and "how my company works"
widens every week, and it bothers them more than any external threat does.

**Goal:** Whole-organization transformation, not a pilot. Wants learning treated as
non-negotiable — willing to commit a large share of team time to it, with near-term
revenue explicitly secondary for the season. Big-swing scoping, not incremental.

**Decision style:** Fast to vision, fast to commit at the top level, then delegates
commercial/operational detail downward to a gatekeeper (see Archetype 2). Doesn't need
to be sold on the *idea* — needs the person doing the work to actually be good.

**What convinces this person:** Proof of work, not a pitch. Inbound interest sparked by
seeing something real get built (not being pitched to build it). Diagnostic accuracy —
correctly naming the problem before being told it — is the trust signal that matters
most, more than credentials or a deck.

**Hard condition:** Whatever gets built has to be runnable by their own people after
handoff. Dependency on an outside party long-term is a dealbreaker, not a feature.

**Emotional payoff when it works:** Relief — that the transformation now rests on more
shoulders than just theirs.

**How to write copy that speaks to this archetype:** Skip the sales framing entirely.
Show the actual mechanism (what setup looks like, what changes day one) rather than
the benefit adjective. Speak to the org-wide ambition, not a narrow use case. Never
imply they need to be managed through this — frame everything as "you and your people
run this," never "we run this for you."

## Archetype 2 — the skeptic gatekeeper

**Profile:** The operational counterpart to Archetype 1 — controls budget, information
flow, and org-structure decisions at the same kind of company. Not the visionary buyer;
the person who has to actually approve, budget, and defend the decision internally.

**Pattern:** Skepticism-until-shown, not hostility. Wants to see a finished plan before
co-creating anything — an open-ended "let's figure it out together" reads as a red
flag, not an invitation. Needs fixed numbers (cost, timeline) rather than open scope.
Treats invisible or unaccounted-for work as actively trust-eroding, not neutral.

**Objections:**
- A chaotic or rushed start.
- Unbounded or creeping cost.
- Anything that could be read as "replacing" existing people or leadership, versus
  "assessing" them — extremely sensitive to this framing distinction specifically.

**What moves this person:** Visible, delivered artifacts inside systems they already
use and control (not a demo environment). Fixed costs and fixed timelines. Being
positioned as the reviewer/assessor of the work, never as its subject. Third-party
proof — evidence from outside the room — before they'll personally vouch for it
internally.

**Communication style that lands:** Plain, concrete, mechanism-first language. "An
agent that checks squad reports every Monday" lands. "Harness the power of AI to
transform your workflows" does not — it reads as content-free and increases suspicion
rather than reducing it.

**Key behavioral note:** This person reacts to finished things. They do not co-imagine
from a blank page — don't ask them to. Bring something concrete to react to.

**How to write copy that speaks to this archetype:** Every claim needs a mechanism
attached. State a fixed scope, not an aspiration. Never use "transform," "unlock," or
"harness" — use verbs that name what actually happens. If inviting this person to
something (an event, a call), tell them exactly what happens and what's asked of them,
with no ambiguity.

## Mapping to the event's target segments

Neither archetype maps 1:1 to a single event segment — they're **decision-making
modes**, not job titles, and often both exist inside the same organization:

- The **founder-visionary** mode maps most closely to the event's "company owner
  seriously committed to moving the whole org forward" segment, and to the
  "tech-forward founder diving deep into AI" segment — both are driven by proof-of-work
  and org-wide ambition, and both need the "your people run this, not us" framing.
- The **skeptic-gatekeeper** mode is less likely to be the applicant themselves at a
  small in-person event, but is often the person an applicant will need to convince
  afterward. Any recap content, follow-up material, or "bring this back to your team"
  copy should anticipate this mode: fixed mechanism, fixed cost/scope, third-party
  proof, never framed as replacing anyone.
- The **technical-team** segment (Slack + GitHub, 2-3+ people) and the **AI-service
  partner** segment don't map cleanly to either archetype — they're closer to peers
  evaluating a tool than stakeholders inside a transformation decision. Copy for them
  should stay in the tone-of-voice guide's default register (technical-credible,
  concrete, no sales framing) rather than leaning on either archetype specifically.
